ETROS Solareon Racing is a student solar racing team based out of Chennai. I joined as a first-year and eventually became Electronics & Coding Head — which is a title that sounds cleaner than it was. In practice it meant being the person responsible for making the car not fail because of software.

The work was real-time embedded firmware in C and C++. The car had sensors everywhere — motor controllers, battery management systems, solar panels — and they all needed to talk to each other over UART and I2C. I built the telemetry pipeline that collected and transmitted live vehicle data during a run. When something broke during testing, it was usually my fault, which is a fast way to learn debugging.

We competed at ESVC-3000 (Electric Solar Vehicle Championship) at a world renowned university (more in secrets). Need to write about achievements here.

I also applied some ML to optimize behavioral cloning inference loops on low-power embedded hardware. That is the part that sounds impressive on paper. The part that actually taught me the most was the telemetry system, the debugging sessions, and two years of not knowing if the car would make it through a test run.

software and mathematics
2026-06-01

An algorithms course here changed something in how I think about programming. The professor made us do math and love math or so I thought, but then he asked us to do something I was afraid of since my 6th grade, to think about math. Think about how would something go and to calculate the entire thought process. I just like listening to what works and reciprocating it, in exams. Thinking is reserved for stuff. Somewhere I missed out but I did train myself to identify problems and their algorithmic needs decently. An algorithm is a formal object. A program is an argument. I was not prepared for how much I would enjoy that framing.

Mathematics, as I understood it growing up, was useful. You learned it because it let you do things. The professor was not interested in that. He was interested in mathematics because it exists — because its truths are true independent of whether anyone ever uses them for anything. That is a different relationship with the subject entirely.

I get anxious when I think of maths like this. This has made mathematics difficult in a specific way — not because I cannot understand it, but because I am sometimes afraid to try. The fear of being wrong about something that is provably correct is its own particular problem. But when a concept does land, it tends to stay. The understanding is durable in a way that rote learning never was.

The implication I keep returning to: a bug is not a mistake. It is a theorem that turned out to be false. The program did exactly what the mathematics said it would. The mathematics was wrong. This is clarifying. It tells you where to look.

A computer is a machine that executes mathematics — imperfectly in practice, which is why you need so many engineers, so many layers, so much verification. I find this clarifying too. Every failure is a place where the mathematics did not hold. The machine is not broken. The proof is.

on the divide
2026-06-01

There is a divide in this field between hardware people and software people. I do not particularly like it. I think it causes both sides to underestimate the other, and occasionally to underestimate themselves.

But I understand why it exists. The domains are genuinely different. The skill sets diverge early. There is enough to learn in either direction to occupy a career, and no obvious reason to cross over once you have picked a lane. It is nobody's fault. It is, by the nature of the thing, what it is.

What I find interesting is the assumption on both sides that the other side is not doing real work. Hardware people sometimes think software is just telling the machine what to do. Software people sometimes think hardware is just moving electrons around. Both are wrong in ways that are almost too obvious to state.

I came up through electronics. I write firmware and kernels now. I do not think of this as having crossed the divide. I think of it as having learned that the divide is less interesting than the machine underneath it.
